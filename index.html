About

This project uses digitised British customs ledgers from the eighteenth century (National Archives series CUST 3). There are 82 volumes from 1696 to 1780. They record goods imported and exported at the Port of London and other ports. They note type and quantity, origin and destination, whether goods were English manufacture or foreign, and whether ships were English or foreign. These tables were written by hand, and had not been converted into a digital format until now. To access the full dataset, please contact Edmond Smith with his email linked here. The visuals below aim to make the source easier to read and explore. By visualising this dataset, we can see broad patterns in trade, shipping, and demand.

Global Flow Map. The globe shows net trade links as arcs. Colour shows direction (blue is a surplus to Britain, red is a deficit). Line width and opacity show scale. You can hover to see names and values. The legend lets you turn groups on and off or isolate one. This joins many small ledger entries into a single view of routes and changing flows.

Annual Composition Snapshot. The bar chart shows one year at a time. Each bar is a place. Height shows the chosen measure. Colour groups broad regions so you can see shifts in balance between Atlantic, European, Asian, African, and prize goods. It mimics opening one volume at one year while still letting you move quickly across time.

Layered Temporal Stream. The stream view stacks yearly totals. Thickness shows volume. Changes in shape show how groups grew or shrank. Because the source splits English manufacture, foreign goods, and carriage by flag, the layers can reveal turning points such as growth of re export trade or new regional weight that is hard to spot in the raw tables.

Trend Lines and Comparison. The line views (3D, 2D, and a smoothed mean) show direction and swings across years. They help pick out war years, policy shifts, bullion movements, or sudden market change. The legend lets you hide minor partners or focus on one path. The mean view softens sharp annual spikes that can come from valuation or reporting variation in the manuscripts so you can see the longer pattern.
